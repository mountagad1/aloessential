In spring, and again after Christmas, "detox" plans and gut supplements fill the shelves. Their packs promise to cleanse, drain and rebalance. The rules on what a food supplement can claim are strict, though. Knowing them helps you sort the useful from the hype.

## Your liver does not need "cleansing"

Your liver and kidneys remove waste and foreign substances all the time. It is not a function that clogs up and needs restarting with a detox plan. That is why "detox", "draining" or "purifying" claims are not among the authorised health claims.

Some nutrients do have authorised claims linked to the liver or metabolism. The most direct concerns **choline**, which "contributes to the maintenance of normal liver function", provided the product supplies at least 82.5 mg per portion. Other claims cover specific steps of metabolism: vitamin B6 contributes to normal cysteine synthesis, molybdenum to normal sulphur amino acid metabolism.

Plants with a reputation for the liver, such as milk thistle or artichoke, have no authorised claim: their claims are still "on hold".

## What really protects your liver

- **Cut down on alcohol**: it is the main avoidable cause of liver damage.
- **Watch your weight and keep moving**: too much fat in the liver is common and linked to inactivity and diet.
- **Eat a varied diet**, with vegetables, fruit, wholegrains and few ultra-processed foods.
- **Be careful with medicines and herbal products**: even over-the-counter ones can strain the liver at high doses. Stick to the dose and tell your GP or pharmacist about everything you take.

## Probiotics: a word labels cannot use freely

Supplements containing lactic acid bacteria and bifidobacteria are very popular. Yet **no health claim has been authorised** for these bacteria in food supplements: the evidence submitted to the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) was judged insufficient. In the UK, the Advertising Standards Authority (ASA) treats the word "probiotic" itself as an implied health claim, which is why some brands say "live bacteria" instead.

That does not mean these products are useless, only that their effects have not been shown to the required standard. A good habit: favour brands that list each strain and its amount, in colony-forming units (CFU), over those that only make promises.

## Your gut loves fibre first

Fibre feeds the bacteria in your gut and keeps things moving. UK government guidelines recommend **30 g of fibre a day** for adults, a target most people do not reach.

To get closer:

1. **Vegetables** at every meal, raw or cooked.
2. **Pulses** (lentils, chickpeas, beans) at least twice a week.
3. **Wholegrains** rather than refined: wholemeal bread, brown rice, porridge oats.
4. **Whole fruit** rather than juice.
5. **Fermented foods** if you like them: yoghurt, kefir, raw sauerkraut.

Increase fibre gradually and drink enough, to avoid bloating in the first few days. Our ideas for starting the day well are in [Healthy breakfast ideas](/en/articles/healthy-breakfast-ideas/).

## Reading the label of a "digestion" or "liver" supplement

- **Find the nutrients** that carry the claims (vitamins, minerals, choline) and their percentage of the nutrient reference values.
- **Pick out the plants** in the formula: their advertised benefits are not authorised claims.
- **Be wary of words** such as "detox", "draining" or "purifying", and of promised results in a few days.
- **For bacteria**, look for the full name of each strain and its amount.

## When to see your GP

Occasional digestive discomfort is common. See your GP if you notice:

- blood in your poo, or black poo;
- unexplained weight loss;
- a change in bowel habits lasting more than a few weeks;
- severe or recurring tummy pain;
- yellowing of the skin or the whites of the eyes.

## In short

Your liver and gut work very well without a "detox". What helps them most are simple habits: less alcohol, more fibre, a varied diet and moving more. A supplement can add to that base, as long as you know how to read what it really promises.

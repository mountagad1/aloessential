Feeling run down? There is no shortage of food supplements promising energy. Behind those promises, though, sits a very precise set of rules. Knowing them helps you read labels with a more critical eye, and avoid missing tiredness that needs more than a course of vitamins.

## A closed list of authorised claims

In Great Britain, as in the EU, a food supplement can only highlight a health effect if it is an **authorised claim**. In GB, authorised claims are those listed on the Great Britain Nutrition and Health Claims Register, under the retained version of Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006. Each claim is tied to a specific nutrient, with wording based on a scientific assessment.

For tiredness, the authorised wording is: "**contributes to the reduction of tiredness and fatigue**". It may only be used for:

- vitamin B2 (riboflavin);
- vitamin B3 (niacin);
- vitamin B5 (pantothenic acid);
- vitamin B6;
- vitamin B9 (folate);
- vitamin B12;
- vitamin C;
- iron;
- magnesium.

Vitamin D, zinc and so-called "energising" plants are not on the list. Ginseng, guarana and rhodiola, for example, have no authorised claim: claims for plants are still "on hold", a status that is not evidence that they work.

## The condition: a significant amount

A claim is only valid if the product provides a **significant amount** of the nutrient. Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 sets this threshold at 15% of the nutrient reference value (NRV) per 100 g, or per portion when the pack contains only one (7.5% per 100 ml for drinks). For a food supplement, in practice, you look at the daily dose.

That is why the label shows a "%NRV" column. Get into the habit of reading it:

- a nutrient shown in large letters on the pack but present at 5% of the NRV cannot carry the claim;
- a very high percentage is not necessarily a benefit: beyond what you need, your body does not make "more energy".

## Read the wording carefully

An authorised claim is about a nutrient, not the product. "Vitamin C contributes to the reduction of tiredness and fatigue" is correct. "This supplement restores your energy in 7 days" is not.

Be wary, too, of promises suggesting that a balanced diet is not enough: they are not allowed. Food supplements should not be used as a substitute for a varied and balanced diet.

## Do not combine without thinking

Vitamins and minerals have safety limits. Taking several supplements at once, or a supplement on top of fortified foods, can take you over those limits without realising. This is especially true of iron, vitamin B6 and vitamin A.

- List what you already take before adding a product.
- Stick to the recommended daily dose.
- Tell your GP or pharmacist about your supplements, especially if you take medicines.

## When tiredness is not about vitamins

Short-term tiredness is often down to too little sleep, stress or a busy period. Looking at your nights and your routine often works better than any supplement: see [Sleep, a pillar of wellbeing](/en/articles/sleep-a-pillar-of-wellbeing/) and our [healthy breakfast ideas](/en/articles/healthy-breakfast-ideas/).

See your GP if your tiredness:

- lasts more than a few weeks despite rest;
- comes with breathlessness, weight loss, a high temperature or bleeding;
- stops you doing your usual activities.

Iron deficiency, for example, is diagnosed with a blood test. Taking supplements "just in case" can hide the problem without solving it.

## In short

Only nine nutrients can legally "contribute to the reduction of tiredness and fatigue", and only when present in a sufficient amount. Read the %NRV column, be wary of promised results, and take lasting tiredness seriously: those are the three habits that count.

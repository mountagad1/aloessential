Aloe vera drinks all look alike on the shelf: a bottle, a green leaf, a promise of wellbeing. What is inside varies a lot. Before you choose, it helps to know what to look for on the label… and what the label is not allowed to promise.

## Gel, juice, concentrate: what are we talking about?

The aloe leaf has two very different parts. The **gel**, at the heart of the leaf, is clear and mostly water. The **latex**, yellow and bitter, runs just under the skin of the leaf: it contains aloin and other hydroxyanthracene derivatives, which have a strong laxative effect.

A serious aloe drink is made from the gel. Depending on the brand, you will find:

- **drinking gels**, where aloe makes up most of the formula;
- more diluted **juices**, sometimes reconstituted from powder;
- **flavoured drinks** where aloe is just one ingredient among others.

None of these is "bad" in itself. What matters is that the label tells you clearly which one you are buying.

## Aloin: what the rules say, and why the UK differs

In 2021, the European Union banned aloe-emodin, emodin and preparations of aloe leaves containing hydroxyanthracene derivatives in food (Regulation 2021/468). In November 2024, the EU General Court annulled the part of that text covering aloe leaf preparations (case T-189/21); the Commission has appealed (case C-38/25 P) and the appeal is still pending. Under EU court rules, the annulment of a regulation only takes effect once an appeal has been dismissed, so where EU law applies the ban still stands for now. The ban on aloe-emodin and emodin itself was not challenged.

There is a reason for this caution. In rats, an extract of the whole aloe vera leaf showed carcinogenic activity, and the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified it as "possibly carcinogenic to humans" (Group 2B). Those studies concerned an extract of the whole leaf, latex included, not gel with the latex removed.

That EU regulation came after Brexit. The version of the rules in force in Great Britain does not include it: the list of prohibited substances on legislation.gov.uk has no aloe entry. Northern Ireland, which follows EU food law on this point, is covered.

In practice, this changes less than it seems. Serious manufacturers on both sides of the Channel use filtered or decolourised gel with these compounds removed, and often say so on the label. Look for that wording: an aloe drink should never be a laxative in disguise. If a product has no ingredient list in English and no UK or EU business address, give it a miss.

## Reading the label in five points

1. **The aloe content.** It is given as a percentage. Check that it refers to the gel, not to an extract or rehydrated powder.
2. **The ingredient list.** Ingredients are listed in decreasing order of quantity. Aloe vera should come first.
3. **Added ingredients.** Honey, fruit juices, plant extracts, vitamins: they can be useful, but they change what the product is. Watch for added sugars and sweeteners too.
4. **The daily amount.** A food supplement states a daily amount not to exceed. It also tells you how long a bottle will last.
5. **Testing.** Some brands have their aloe content and quality certified by an independent body such as the International Aloe Science Council. It is a good sign of seriousness, not a guarantee that the product works.

## What the label can (and cannot) promise

In Great Britain, health claims on foods and supplements must appear on the GB Nutrition and Health Claims Register. No claim specific to aloe vera is authorised. Some plant ("botanical") claims are formally "on hold" while they wait to be assessed; that status is not evidence that they work.

When a drink highlights a specific benefit, it is therefore usually thanks to an **added vitamin or mineral**. For example, "vitamin C contributes to the normal function of the immune system" is an authorised claim, provided the product contains a significant amount. The benefit then comes from the vitamin, not from the aloe.

Be wary of promises to "detox", "cleanse" the body or prevent disease: they are not allowed and have no solid basis. We look at what the studies say in [our article on aloe vera juice](/en/articles/aloe-vera-juice-what-science-says/).

## How to take it day to day

### How much?

The amount on the label, and no more. When the drink is sold as a food supplement, the recommended daily amount must be shown on the pack. It varies from one product to another: neat or diluted in water, as the manufacturer recommends. Start with a small amount if you have a sensitive stomach.

### Morning, evening, before meals?

You often read that aloe should be taken on an empty stomach or before meals "to absorb it better". No study shows that one time of day is better than another. Follow the manufacturer's directions, and pick the time you are most likely to remember.

### How long does a bottle last?

Divide the bottle's volume by the daily amount. A 1-litre bottle taken at 3 × 30 ml a day (90 ml) lasts about ten days; at 30 ml a day, more than a month.

Then compare that with the "once opened, use within" period on the label, often in the fridge. If, at your dose, the bottle lasts longer than that, a smaller size suits you better. The same sum helps you compare prices: work out the cost per daily dose rather than per litre.

### What does it taste like?

Plain gel tastes vegetal, sometimes slightly bitter or sharp depending on the product, and not everyone likes it. Flavoured versions soften it with fruit juice, honey or flavourings: check the sugars per serving. Diluting it in a glass of water also tones the taste down.

### A "course": for how long?

Brands often suggest "courses" lasting several weeks. Those lengths are not based on studies, and no health claim specific to aloe is authorised. If you drink it regularly, choose a product made from filtered gel, and talk to your GP or pharmacist if you take medication or if a digestive problem persists.

## Who it is for, and who should avoid it

An aloe drink can be part of a varied diet, as a daily habit you enjoy. It is not suitable for everyone:

- avoid it during pregnancy and breastfeeding, and for children, unless a doctor advises otherwise;
- ask your GP or pharmacist if you take any medication;
- stop if it upsets your stomach.

## In short

A good aloe drink has a clear label: the form of aloe used, how much of it there is, added ingredients that make sense, and claims based on identified nutrients. The rest is marketing.

When we think about staying hydrated, drinks come to mind first. Yet a large share of the water we take in comes from food: about 20 to 30%, according to the estimate used by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA).

## The water champions

Most fresh vegetables and many fruits are more than 85% water, some more than 95%. A few examples, from the French Ciqual food composition table (Anses):

| Food | Water per 100 g |
| --- | --- |
| Radishes | 95.5 g |
| Lettuce, raw | 95.4 g |
| Cucumber, raw | 95.2 g |
| Courgette, raw | 94.8 g |
| Tomato, raw | 94.6 g |
| Celery, raw | 93.4 g |
| Watermelon | 91 g |
| Strawberries | 90.3 g |
| Grapefruit | 90 g |
| Orange | 87.3 g |
| Cantaloupe melon | 84.2 g |

They also provide fibre, vitamins and minerals, including potassium.

The full table, with the water in a usual portion of each food, is on our page [How much water should you drink a day?](/en/how-much-water-per-day/).

## Easy everyday ideas

**At breakfast**, add orange segments or a few strawberries. **At lunch**, a cucumber salad with yoghurt and mint takes five minutes. **As a snack**, celery or radish sticks make a good swap for biscuits.

## In winter too

Vegetable soups and broths are a great way to stay hydrated when it is cold. Citrus fruit, in season, brings freshness and vitamin C.

## Water is still the basis

Food adds to your hydration; it does not replace drinking. Drink regularly through the day, without waiting until you are thirsty, and more when it is hot or you are active.

How much should you drink in total? Our [water-per-day calculator](/en/how-much-water-per-day/) gives the European reference values by age, pregnancy or breastfeeding.

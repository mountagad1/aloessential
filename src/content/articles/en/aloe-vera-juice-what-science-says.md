Aloe vera juice has found its place on the wellbeing shelf. But between marketing claims and scientific data, the gap is sometimes wide.

## Gel and latex: an essential distinction

The aloe leaf contains two very different substances. The **gel**, in the centre, is mild and full of water. The **latex**, yellow and bitter, sits just under the skin of the leaf: it contains aloin, a powerful laxative.

The European Union has restricted hydroxyanthracene derivatives, including aloin-related compounds, in food and supplements. Great Britain did not adopt that 2021 rule, but the advice is the same everywhere: a good-quality juice should be decolourised and low in aloin.

## Digestion: modest results so far

A few small studies suggest that aloe gel may help with some digestive discomfort. The samples are small and the methods vary: it is too early to talk about proven effectiveness.

## "Detox": a promise without foundation

Your liver and kidneys already remove waste from the body. No juice "cleanses" you: this claim is not authorised.

## Precautions

- Avoid aloe juice during pregnancy and breastfeeding.
- Ask your GP if you take medication, particularly for diabetes or heart conditions: interactions are possible.
- Stop if you get digestive problems.

## Our view

Aloe juice can be an occasional part of a varied diet, as long as you choose a tested product. It does not replace a balanced diet or medical advice. To read the label well, see [Aloe vera drinks: how to choose one](/en/articles/aloe-vera-drinks-how-to-choose/).
